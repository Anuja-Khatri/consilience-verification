\begin{abstract}
I present a deterministic, typed engine that compares scientific claims across theories: each claim is
reduced to typed fields (entity\_type, axis, polarity, value) and a fixed rule-based judge assigns a
correspondence grade or a fork. The judge is reproducible, provable and auditable. I re-express it as a
logic program (verdict equivalence \textbf{1.0}, $n = 213$ pairs), as a first-order theory under an SMT
solver (congruence \textbf{1.0}, $n = 311$ verdicts), with machine-checked proof objects (coverage
\textbf{0.9842}, $n = 316$) and \textbf{0} integrity violations ($n = 316$). On the discovery side the
engine recovers the COGITATE adversarial-collaboration key at \textbf{2 of 3} predictions (revised;
$n = 3$; not significant against a 1000-key permutation null, $p = 0.075$). The eLife synergistic-workspace
key recovery is below chance: \textbf{0 exact / 6 partial of 14} validated rows, empirical $p = 0.981$
($n = 1000$; random keys recover more). The method's limits are first-class results: both key recoveries
are reported with their chance rates, single-pass extraction carries a measured noise floor, and the
extraction front-end under-propagates negation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Comparing scientific claims across competing theories is usually done by hand and by argument. This paper
presents a deterministic engine that does it mechanically and auditably. Each claim is reduced to a small
typed record (entity type, axis, polarity, value), and a fixed rule-based judge compares two claims from
the typed fields alone, returning a correspondence grade or a fork. Because the judge is a function of
typed fields, every comparison is reproducible, and the judge can be re-expressed as a logic program, as a
first-order theory under an SMT solver \cite{z3}, and as machine-checked proof objects. This division of
labour, a language model proposing typed candidates and a sound external program disposing, follows the
LLM-Modulo pattern of a generator paired with verifiers it cannot talk its way past \cite{kambhampati2024}.

I exercise the engine on a frozen corpus of six theories of consciousness, with bridge extensions that link
active inference \cite{buckley2017} to machine learning (control as probabilistic inference
\cite{levine2018}; predictive coding as an approximation to backpropagation \cite{millidge2022}) and that
span integrated information theory in its current formulation \cite{albantakis2023}; nothing in the method
is specific to that domain. I report two kinds of result. On the discovery side, the engine re-adjudicates
a founding cross-field correspondence record and attempts to recover two held-out, published answer keys,
the COGITATE adversarial collaboration \cite{cogitate2025} and the eLife synergistic-workspace bridge
\cite{luppi2024}, each scored against an explicit permutation chance rate. On the verification side, the
judge's decisions are a transparent, checkable function of the typed fields, and a preregistered
statement-level perturbation suite measures how the extraction front-end behaves under meaning-preserving
and meaning-flipping edits. Throughout, I report the method's limits as first-class results: discovery
claims carry their chance rates, including an honest below-chance negative; single-pass extraction carries
a measured noise floor; and the under-propagation of negation is stated plainly. All figures render from a
single numbers-locked registry snapshot.

\section{Discovery}

\subsection{Cross-field re-adjudication}
Before this evaluation, the engine's earliest corpus was a set of correspondences asserted across physics,
neuroscience and contemplative texts. I chose that corpus deliberately: it is where the vocabularies are
most alien to one another, the hardest place for surface wording to do the work, and where the original
claims were made with the least discipline. The re-adjudication test asks a simple
question: if the earliest asserted correspondences are re-typed under today's frozen instrument (v2) and
judged by today's fixed rules, how many survive? Re-running every pair end to end through the deterministic
judge: of the pairs that carry a recorded prior grade ($n = 7$), the new grade agrees with the old one for
a single pair (agreement \textbf{0.1429}, that is 1 of 7) and downgrades or withdraws the other six
(\textbf{0.8571}, 6 of 7). I read this as the method working rather than failing: most originally asserted
cross-field correspondences do not survive re-typing under a fixed codebook, and a judge strict enough to
retire most of its builder's own early claims is what makes its surviving claims worth attention.

\subsection{Unification index}
The unification index is a ranking: it orders the correspondences the engine has persisted by how much
unifying work each one does, so that the pairs tying the most theories together rise to the top. A ranking
like this is only useful if it is stable, and the worry is that the order is an artifact of the particular
weights I chose when combining its ingredients. The weight-simplex test recomputes the ranking under every
weighting across the simplex and asks whether the order holds. I scored
each persisted node correspondence by a preregistered combination of forks-touched, support-cone and
axis-reach (weights 1/1/1), then swept the weights across the simplex and measured Kendall's $\tau$
\cite{kendall1938}, the rank correlation between the base ranking and each reweighted one. Across the 216
correspondences in the ranking, the minimum $\tau$ over the simplex sweep is
\textbf{0.7165} ($n = 216$). I report this as moderately weight-sensitive rather than robust: the order is
largely but not entirely weight-independent, and the generative grade dominates the score, which I flag.

\subsection{Held-out key recoveries, with chance rates}
COGITATE is an adversarial collaboration in which proponents of two rival theories of consciousness,
integrated information theory and global neuronal workspace theory, negotiated in advance, over several
years, a shared set of predictions and the experiments that would adjudicate them \cite{cogitate2025}. That
negotiated key is the right benchmark precisely because it was so expensive to produce: if the engine,
reading only the theories' own texts, recovers predictions that took years of expert argument to fix, the
typed judge is doing something surface text alone does not hand it. The key was
preregistered before the collaboration's results were known \cite{cogitate_prereg}, and the engine never
ingested the key text, so the holdout is clean. Running the v2 instrument end to end against the three
held-out predictions, the engine recovers \textbf{2 of 3} (revised gated result \{2,0,1,9\}; $n = 3$).
Outcome-informed disclosure (verbatim): ``iit\_c009 was initially rejected at the gate; after a sensitivity
check showed it was the sole binding claim for P3, it was re-reviewed and approved on codebook-consistency
grounds (matching approved canonical iit\_c025). Original gated result \{1,0,2,9\} and revised \{2,0,1,9\}
are both reported; fully ungated sensitivity \{2,0,1,16\}.'' The one remaining miss (P2, temporal\_profile)
is a property of the instrument rather than the gate: no IIT temporal\_profile claim was ever extracted. To
judge whether recovering two of three is meaningful, I compared it against a null of 1000 random typed-row
keys; the fraction recovering at least as much as the real key is the empirical permutation
\textbf{$p = 0.075$} ($n = 1000$), beside a normal approximation $z = 2.2693$ from the same
recovered-count null. Because that null is discrete and right-skewed, the $z$ overstates significance and
the empirical \textbf{$p = 0.075$} ($n = 1000$) governs: it does not reach $\alpha = 0.05$, so I read the
COGITATE recovery as suggestive only.

The eLife bridge is a second, independent key of a different kind: a published, information-theoretic
analysis that formalises a quantitative bridge between the same two theories, deriving where integrated
information and the global workspace should correspond \cite{luppi2024}. Built by different authors with
different machinery than COGITATE, it is an independent check against a key no one on the engine side
wrote. I diffed the engine's
IIT$\times$GNWT fork and correspondence set against the \textbf{14 validated} bridge rows. Across those 14
rows the engine recovers \textbf{0 exact} and \textbf{6 partial} ($n = 14$) and misses 8, with \textbf{0}
rows unmappable to any engine axis ($n = 14$; the one vocabulary-gap row in the provisional 16-row build
was among the 2 dropped at the format gate as methods, so the validated denominator of 14 carries 0
unmappable). Scored against the same 1000-key null, the result does not beat chance: random keys recover
more on average (null mean 9.2), and the fraction of null keys doing at least as well is empirical
\textbf{$p = 0.981$} ($n = 1000$), below the null. I report this as found: on the engine's IIT$\times$GNWT
set the eLife bridge's partial recovery is at or below chance.

\section{Verification}

\subsection{Audit method}
Can the engine's own earlier discoveries withstand a strict, rule-based audit? Before trusting any
correspondence the engine surfaces, I wanted to know how many of its prior demonstration discoveries
survive explicit criteria rather than impression. I applied a five-part criterion audit to each,
checking provenance, quote-support, a Newman screen, testability and scale, and failing any discovery
that misses even one criterion. Of the 9 demonstration discoveries, \textbf{1 of 9} survives all five
criteria ($n = 9$); of a separate sample of 30 engine-asserted matches, \textbf{2 of 30} survive
($n = 30$). The audit is deliberately strict: most prior discoveries fail at least one criterion, which I
treat as the audit doing its job.

\subsection{Instrument}
How much does the extraction front-end disagree with itself when the input has not changed? Every
downstream invariance and tracking figure is only interpretable against that noise floor, so I fix one
estimate of it and use it throughout. The adopted decoding is \texttt{baseline-single-pass}, and
I estimate self-consistency with the sprint estimator over repeated runs of the same passages. Across the
88 repeated-run units the sprint estimator puts standing self-consistency at \textbf{0.25}
(95\% CI [0.1713, 0.3496], $n = 88$); this is the measured noise floor against which every downstream
contrast is scored. For comparison only, the prior Task-5 estimator on the same 80 runs reports 0.271, a
coarser cluster unit, so a noise floor this high is itself a limitation that every invariance number below
is read against.

\subsection{The verified judge}
Can the judge's rules be proved correct rather than merely trusted? Because the judge is a function of the
typed fields alone, independent formal re-expressions of the same rules should reproduce its verdicts
exactly, making each verdict a checkable consequence of the inputs rather than a behaviour of one program.
I re-expressed the judge three ways and compared each against it over the corpus: as a logic program, as a
first-order theory discharged by the Z3 SMT solver \cite{z3}, and as machine-checked proof objects in the
spirit of formalised-mathematics libraries \cite{mathlib}. Across the 213 theory pairs in the corpus the
logic program returns the identical verdict in every case (verdict equivalence \textbf{1.0}, $n = 213$);
across the 311 verdicts checked under the solver, congruence is \textbf{1.0} ($n = 311$); proof objects
cover \textbf{0.9842} of the 316 derivations ($n = 316$), with \textbf{0} integrity violations across those
same 316 ($n = 316$). Three re-expressions agreeing exactly is the result the verification lane rests on:
the verdicts are provable from their inputs, not taken on trust.

\subsection{Statement-level contracts: the negation headline}
What happens to a verdict when a sentence's meaning is flipped by negation? A typed judge is only
trustworthy if the fields it reads track meaning, so I ran a preregistered, statement-level perturbation
suite that applies meaning-preserving and meaning-flipping edits and measures how the typed fields and
verdicts respond against a re-extraction noise-floor control (OSF preregistration DOI
\href{https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/5N24M}{10.17605/OSF.IO/5N24M}). One caveat frames the polarity
numbers: the claim store is near single-class on polarity (92 asserts, 3 denies), so the identity-control
polarity stability \textbf{0.975} ($n = 40$) and the panel polarity agreement below largely reflect the
extractor's default rather than demonstrated polarity reading, which is why the negation figure is the
informative one. Over the 34 valid explicit-negation items,
negation flips the extracted polarity only \textbf{0.1765} of the time ($n = 34$); over the 8 valid
double-negation items the extractor restores the assertion \textbf{0.0} of the time ($n = 8$, low-$n$,
since the perturber mostly fails to negate twice); and verdict tracking under the L2 edits is correct for
\textbf{0.4189} of the 74 items ($n = 74$). The deterministic judge is correct given its fields; what these
numbers locate is the extraction front-end under-propagating negation into those fields. A single-reviewer
hand-check agrees with the automated verifier on \textbf{0.893} of 28 items ($n = 28$), so the gap is in
extraction, not the check.

\subsection{Obfuscation control}
Does hiding each theory's coined vocabulary change the engine's verdicts? If the correspondences were
driven by surface wording, masking the signature terms should degrade them; if by the typed fields,
masking should leave them intact. I ran two arms of extraction and fork recovery, identical except that
one operated on passages with the theories' surface vocabulary obfuscated, recording both the change in
recovery and whether the extractor re-emitted the coined terms. Across the 14 canonical forks, obfuscation
moves fork
recovery by only \textbf{0.072} ($n = 14$), so surface masking does not reduce fork recovery. It does not,
however, blind the model to theory identity: over those same 14 the extractor re-identifies the theories
and re-emits their coined terms, producing \textbf{12} de-obfuscation leaks ($n = 14$): the verdicts
survive obfuscation, but the model still recognises which theories it reads.

\subsection{Reliability framing}
Do two different vendors' models type the same claims the same way? Cross-vendor agreement speaks to
whether the typing protocol is reproducible across independent models, not to whether its verdicts are
valid. I ran a two-vendor panel (gpt-4.1 $\times$ gemini-3.1-pro-preview, full coverage) and measured
inter-model agreement on each typed field. Over the 95 claims typed by both vendors, cross-vendor
agreement is \textbf{0.525} on entity
type (Cohen's $\kappa$ \cite{cohen1960}, $n = 95$), \textbf{0.503} on axis ($\kappa$, $n = 95$), and
\textbf{0.811} on polarity (PABAK \cite{byrt1993}, $n = 95$); the polarity PABAK carries the single-class
caveat noted for the negation headline above. Standing self-consistency within a single model is the
adopted stability \textbf{0.25} (sprint estimator, 95\% CI [0.1713, 0.3496], $n = 88$; Task-5 estimator
0.271 for comparison). Moderate cross-vendor agreement shows the protocol is reproducible across models,
not that its typing is valid.

\section{Limitations}
These limitations import the pre-sprint evaluation's, which this paper supersedes under the lock. The
engine's six theories are all theories of consciousness, so results may not transfer to other fields;
cross-field reach rests on the re-adjudication record and the corpus extensions, not the core. The
reference labels were produced by the builder, so the judge-after-audit reference reflects one builder's
choices; a second, independent annotator is planned but not yet run, so human agreement, the ceiling
against which model agreement is read, is not yet available. In production a smaller same-family model
cross-checks the main extractor; the two-vendor panel is an evaluation add-on measuring reproducibility,
not validity. The corpus is small (95 approved claims, six theories); the deeper arms, embedding
alignment, unsupervised optimal transport via Gromov-Wasserstein
couplings \cite{memoli2011}, and partial-information decomposition of the verdict \cite{williams_beer2010},
are pilot-scale feasibility studies in a planned second paper.

Single-pass extraction carries a measured noise floor (stability \textbf{0.25}, $n = 88$), so every
invariance and tracking number is reported relative to the identity control, and negation tracking is
itself a measured limitation. The store is near single-class on polarity (92 asserts / 3 denies), so
polarity-stability figures reflect the extractor's default rather than demonstrated polarity reading.
Holdout controls the prompt, not the training data: held-out keys never entered extraction or judging
prompts (measured clean) but may sit in a model's pretraining, which the obfuscation control, showing
identity is not blinded, addresses. Several review packets were judged at the
statement level or from truncated excerpts without full fork-side source text, so those arms are reported
as ``no fork flagged by the scan'', not as validation of the forks. Decisions were model-drafted
suggestions (Claude) reviewed and confirmed by the author, and the open-weight panel leg is not run (no
hosted-open-weight key), so the panel is two-vendor.

\section{Conclusion}
The engine turns cross-theory claim comparison into a reproducible, auditable computation: a typed
intermediate representation, a deterministic judge, and three independent re-expressions of it that agree
exactly. Its discovery claims are modest by construction: most prior cross-field correspondences do
not survive re-typing, one held-out key is recovered only suggestively, and the other below chance. I
report them that way. The verification lane, not the discovery headline, is the contribution I stand
behind: a claim-comparison verdict that is a checkable function of its inputs.
