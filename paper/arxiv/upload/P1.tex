\begin{abstract}
I present a deterministic engine that compares scientific claims across competing theories. Each claim is
first reduced to a fixed schema of typed fields, in the style of information extraction: its entity type,
its axis (the aspect of the system the claim addresses), its polarity (assertion versus negation), and a
value. A deterministic rule-based verifier, which I call the judge, then compares two claims from these
fields alone. Its output, the verdict, is either a grade of correspondence, an ordinal label for how
strongly the two claims agree, or a fork, a question on which the theories make mutually incompatible
commitments. The judge is reproducible, provable and auditable. I re-express it as a logic program (verdict
equivalence \textbf{1.0}, $n = 213$ pairs), as a first-order theory under an SMT solver (congruence
\textbf{1.0}, $n = 311$ verdicts), and as machine-checked proof objects (coverage \textbf{0.9842},
$n = 316$), with \textbf{0} integrity violations ($n = 316$). On the discovery side the engine recovers the
COGITATE adversarial-collaboration key at \textbf{2 of 3} predictions (revised; $n = 3$; not significant
against a 1000-key permutation null, $p = 0.075$). The eLife synergistic-workspace key recovery is below
chance: \textbf{0 exact / 6 partial of 14} validated rows, with empirical $p = 0.981$ ($n = 1000$; random
keys recover more). The method's limits are first-class results: both key recoveries are reported with
their chance rates, single-pass extraction carries a measured noise floor (its test-retest reliability
across repeated runs), and the extraction front-end under-propagates negation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Comparing scientific claims across competing theories is usually done by hand and by argument. This paper
presents a deterministic engine that does it mechanically and auditably. Each claim is reduced to a small
schema of typed fields (entity type, axis, polarity, value), in the style of information extraction, and a
deterministic rule-based verifier, the judge, compares two claims from those fields alone, returning a
grade of correspondence or a fork. Because the judge is a function of the typed fields, every comparison is
reproducible, and the judge can be re-expressed as a logic program, as a first-order theory under an SMT
solver \cite{z3}, and as machine-checked proof objects. This division of labour, a language model proposing
typed candidates and a sound external program disposing, follows the LLM-Modulo pattern of a generator
paired with verifiers it cannot talk its way past \cite{kambhampati2024}. The judge reads only the typed
fields, so the generator has no text channel through which to argue a verdict into being.

I exercise the engine on a versioned, immutable dataset of six theories of consciousness (the frozen
corpus). Bridge extensions link active inference \cite{buckley2017} to machine learning, through control as
probabilistic inference \cite{levine2018} and predictive coding as an approximation to backpropagation
\cite{millidge2022}, and span integrated information theory in its current formulation
\cite{albantakis2023}; nothing in the method is specific to that domain. Throughout, the extraction
front-end (its model, prompts and decoding settings) is held fixed and versioned, and I call this frozen
pipeline the instrument. I report two kinds of result. On the discovery side, the engine re-adjudicates a
founding cross-field correspondence record and attempts to recover two published answer keys: the COGITATE
adversarial collaboration \cite{cogitate2025} and the eLife synergistic-workspace bridge \cite{luppi2024}.
These keys are held out from the engine's prompts, though they may sit in the underlying models'
pretraining, and each recovery is scored against an explicit permutation chance rate. On the verification
side, the judge's decisions are a transparent, checkable function of the typed fields, and a preregistered
statement-level perturbation suite measures how the instrument behaves under meaning-preserving and
meaning-flipping edits.

The verification lane is the contribution I stand behind, and the discovery results are deliberately
modest. I report the method's limits as first-class results: both key recoveries carry their chance rates,
including an honest below-chance negative; single-pass extraction carries a measured noise floor; and the
under-propagation of negation is stated plainly. Every figure in the paper renders from a single
numbers-locked registry snapshot, so the same quantity never drifts from one document to the next.

\section{Discovery}

\subsection{Cross-field re-adjudication}
Before this evaluation, the engine's earliest corpus was a set of correspondences asserted across physics,
neuroscience and contemplative texts. I chose that corpus deliberately: it is where the vocabularies are
most alien to one another, the hardest place for surface wording to do the work, and where the original
claims were made with the least discipline. The re-adjudication test asks a simple
question: if the earliest asserted correspondences are re-typed under today's frozen instrument (v2) and
judged by today's fixed rules, how many survive? I re-ran every pair end to end through the deterministic
judge. Of the pairs that carry a recorded prior grade ($n = 7$), the new grade agrees with the old one for
a single pair (agreement \textbf{0.1429}, that is 1 of 7) and downgrades or withdraws the other six
(\textbf{0.8571}, 6 of 7). I read this as the method working rather than failing: most originally asserted
cross-field correspondences do not survive re-typing under a fixed codebook (the judge's rule set and field
definitions). A judge strict enough to retire most of its builder's own early claims is what makes its
surviving claims worth attention.

\subsection{Unification index}
The unification index is a ranking. It orders the correspondences the engine has persisted (the claim pairs
the judge graded as matching) by how much unifying work each one does, so the pairs that tie the most
theories together rise to the top. A ranking like this is only useful if it is stable, and the worry is
that the order is an artifact of the particular weights I chose when combining its ingredients. The
weight-simplex test checks that worry: it recomputes the ranking under every weighting across the simplex
and asks whether the order holds. I scored each persisted correspondence by a preregistered combination of
three ingredients (how many forks it touches, the size of its supporting evidence cone, and how many axes
it reaches), with weights 1/1/1. I then swept the weights across the simplex and measured Kendall's $\tau$
\cite{kendall1938}, the rank correlation between the base ranking and each reweighted one. Across the 216
correspondences in the ranking, the minimum $\tau$ over the sweep is \textbf{0.7165} ($n = 216$). I report
this as moderately weight-sensitive rather than robust: the order is largely but not entirely
weight-independent, and one ingredient dominates the score, which I flag.

\subsection{Held-out key recoveries, with chance rates}
COGITATE is an adversarial collaboration between proponents of two rival theories of consciousness,
integrated information theory and global neuronal workspace theory (IIT and GNWT, respectively). Over
several years they negotiated, in advance, a shared set of predictions and the experiments that would
adjudicate them
\cite{cogitate2025}. That negotiated key is the right benchmark precisely because it was so expensive to
produce: if the engine, reading only the theories' own texts, recovers predictions that took years of
expert argument to fix, the judge is doing something the surface text alone does not hand it. The key was
preregistered before the collaboration's results were known \cite{cogitate_prereg}, and the engine never
ingested the key text, so the holdout is clean. I ran the instrument end to end against the three held-out
predictions and then applied the review gate, which accepts or rejects each extracted claim. The engine
recovers \textbf{2 of 3} predictions; the full counts of recovered, partial, missed and beyond-key claims
are \{2,0,1,9\} ($n = 3$). Outcome-informed disclosure (verbatim): ``iit\_c009 was initially rejected at
the gate; after a sensitivity check showed it was the sole binding claim for P3, it was re-reviewed and
approved on codebook-consistency grounds (matching approved canonical iit\_c025). Original gated result
\{1,0,2,9\} and revised \{2,0,1,9\} are both reported; fully ungated sensitivity \{2,0,1,16\}.'' The one
remaining miss, the second prediction (P2, on temporal profile), is a property of the instrument rather
than the gate: no IIT temporal\_profile claim was ever extracted. To judge whether recovering two of three
is meaningful, I compared it against a null of 1000 random typed-row keys. The fraction of those keys that
recover at least as much as the real key is the empirical permutation $p$, here \textbf{$p = 0.075$}
($n = 1000$), beside a normal approximation $z = 2.2693$ from the same null. Because that null is discrete
and right-skewed, the $z$ overstates significance and the empirical \textbf{$p = 0.075$} ($n = 1000$)
governs: it does not reach $\alpha = 0.05$, so I read the COGITATE recovery as suggestive only.

The eLife bridge is a second, independent key of a different kind: a published, information-theoretic
analysis that formalises a quantitative bridge between the same two theories, deriving where integrated
information and the global workspace should correspond \cite{luppi2024}. Built by different authors with
different machinery than COGITATE, it is an independent check against a key no one on the engine side
wrote. I compared the engine's IIT$\times$GNWT fork and correspondence set against the \textbf{14 validated}
bridge rows. Across those 14 rows the engine recovers \textbf{0 exact} matches and \textbf{6 partial} ones
($n = 14$) and misses 8, with \textbf{0} rows unmappable to any engine axis ($n = 14$). (The one
vocabulary-gap row in the provisional 16-row build was among the 2 dropped at the format gate as methods,
so the validated denominator of 14 carries 0 unmappable.) Scored against the same 1000-key null, the result
does not beat chance: the random keys recover more on average (null mean 9.2), and the fraction of null
keys doing at least as well as the engine is empirical \textbf{$p = 0.981$} ($n = 1000$), placing the
engine below the null. I report this as found: on the engine's IIT$\times$GNWT set, the eLife bridge's
partial recovery is at or below chance.

\section{Verification}

\subsection{Audit method}
Which of the engine's own earlier discoveries survive a strict audit? Before trusting any correspondence
the engine surfaces, I wanted to know how many of its prior demonstration discoveries hold up under
explicit criteria rather than impression. I applied a five-part criterion audit to each discovery,
checking provenance, quote-support, a screen for superficial analogy (the Newman screen), testability and
scale, and failing any discovery that misses even one criterion. Of the 9 demonstration discoveries,
\textbf{1 of 9} survives all five criteria ($n = 9$); of a separate sample of 30 engine-asserted matches,
\textbf{2 of 30} survive ($n = 30$). The audit is deliberately strict: most prior discoveries fail at least
one criterion, which I treat as the audit doing its job.

\subsection{Instrument}
How much does the extraction front-end disagree with itself when the input has not changed? This is the
test-retest reliability of the instrument, its self-consistency across repeated runs, which I report as the
stability. Every downstream invariance and tracking figure is only interpretable against that floor, so I
fix one estimate of it and use it throughout, scoring each contrast against the measured floor rather than
against an idealised assumption of zero noise. The adopted decoding is \texttt{baseline-single-pass}, and I
estimate the stability with the primary estimator over repeated runs of the same passages. Across the 88
repeated-run units the primary estimator puts the stability at \textbf{0.25} (95\% CI [0.1713, 0.3496],
$n = 88$). For comparison only, an earlier, coarser estimator on the same 80 runs reports 0.271. A noise
floor this high is itself a limitation, and every invariance number below is read against it.

\subsection{The verified judge}
Can the judge's rules be proved correct rather than merely trusted? Because the judge is a function of the
typed fields alone, independent formal re-expressions of the same rules should reproduce its verdicts
exactly, making each verdict a checkable consequence of the inputs rather than a behaviour of one program.
I re-expressed the judge three ways and compared each against it over the corpus: as a logic program, as a
first-order theory discharged by the Z3 SMT solver \cite{z3}, and as machine-checked proof objects in the
spirit of formalised-mathematics libraries \cite{mathlib}. Across the 213 theory pairs in the corpus, the
logic program returns the identical verdict in every case (verdict equivalence \textbf{1.0}, $n = 213$).
Under the solver, the first-order theory is congruent with the judge on all the verdicts checked
(\textbf{1.0}, $n = 311$). The proof objects cover \textbf{0.9842} of the 316 derivations ($n = 316$), with
\textbf{0} integrity violations across those same 316 ($n = 316$). Three independent re-expressions agreeing
exactly is the result the verification lane rests on: the verdicts are provable from their inputs, not
taken on trust.

\subsection{Statement-level contracts: the negation headline}
What happens to a verdict when a sentence's meaning is flipped by negation? The judge is only trustworthy
if the fields it reads track meaning, so I ran a preregistered, statement-level perturbation suite. The
suite applies meaning-preserving and meaning-flipping edits to each passage and measures how the typed
fields and the verdicts respond, scored against a re-extraction noise-floor control (OSF preregistration
DOI \href{https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/5N24M}{10.17605/OSF.IO/5N24M}). One caveat frames the polarity
numbers. The claim store, the versioned, immutable dataset of extracted claims, is near single-class on
polarity, that is on assertion versus negation (92 asserts, 3 denies). Because of that imbalance, the
polarity stability under an identity control (the same passage re-extracted with no edit) is \textbf{0.975}
($n = 40$). The panel polarity agreement reported below largely reflects the extractor's default of
assertion rather than a demonstrated reading of polarity, so the negation figure is the informative one. Under
explicit negation, the extracted polarity flips as it should only \textbf{0.1765} of the time ($n = 34$).
Under double negation, the extractor restores the assertion \textbf{0.0} of the time ($n = 8$, a low count
because the perturber mostly fails to negate twice). And the verdict tracks the edit correctly, meaning its
output label changes as it should, for \textbf{0.4189} of the 74 meaning-flipping edits ($n = 74$). The
deterministic judge is correct given its fields; what these numbers locate is the extraction front-end
under-propagating negation into those fields. A single-reviewer hand-check agrees with the judge's
automated verification on \textbf{0.893} of 28 items ($n = 28$), so the gap is in extraction, not in the
check.

\subsection{Obfuscation control}
Does hiding each theory's coined vocabulary change the engine's verdicts? If the correspondences were
driven by surface wording, masking the signature terms should degrade them; if by the typed fields,
masking should leave them intact. I ran two arms of extraction and fork recovery, identical except that one
operated on passages with the theories' surface vocabulary obfuscated, and I recorded both the change in
recovery and whether the extractor re-emitted the coined terms. Fork recovery is the share of the known
forks the engine reproduces. Across the 14 reference forks, obfuscation moves it by only \textbf{0.072}
($n = 14$), so surface masking does not reduce fork recovery. It does not, however, blind the model to
theory identity: over those same 14 the extractor re-identifies the theories and re-emits their coined
terms, producing \textbf{12} such leaks ($n = 14$). The verdicts survive obfuscation, but the model still
recognises which theories it reads.

\subsection{Reliability framing}
Do two different vendors' models type the same claims the same way? Cross-vendor agreement speaks to
whether the typing protocol is reproducible across independent models, not to whether its verdicts are
valid. I ran a two-vendor panel (gpt-4.1 $\times$ gemini-3.1-pro-preview, full coverage) and measured
inter-model agreement on each typed field, over the 95 claims typed by both vendors. On entity type the
agreement is \textbf{0.525} (Cohen's $\kappa$ \cite{cohen1960}, $n = 95$) and on axis it is \textbf{0.503}
($\kappa$, $n = 95$). On polarity it is \textbf{0.811} (the prevalence- and bias-adjusted kappa, PABAK
\cite{byrt1993}, $n = 95$), though this figure carries the single-class caveat noted for the negation
headline above. Within a single model, the stability is \textbf{0.25} (the primary estimator, 95\% CI
[0.1713, 0.3496], $n = 88$; an earlier, coarser estimator gives 0.271). Moderate cross-vendor agreement
shows the protocol is reproducible across models, not that its typing is valid.

\section{Limitations}
These limitations carry over from an earlier internal evaluation that this paper supersedes. The
engine's six theories are all theories of consciousness, so results may not transfer to other fields;
cross-field reach rests on the re-adjudication record and the corpus extensions, not the core. The
reference labels were produced by the builder, so the judge-after-audit reference reflects one builder's
choices; a second, independent annotator is planned but not yet run, so human agreement, the ceiling
against which model agreement is read, is not yet available. In production a smaller same-family model
cross-checks the main extractor; the two-vendor panel is an evaluation add-on measuring reproducibility,
not validity. The corpus is small (95 approved claims, six theories); the deeper arms, embedding
alignment, unsupervised optimal transport via Gromov-Wasserstein
couplings \cite{memoli2011}, and partial-information decomposition of the verdict \cite{williams_beer2010},
are pilot-scale feasibility studies in a planned second paper.

Single-pass extraction carries a measured noise floor (stability \textbf{0.25}, $n = 88$), so every
invariance and tracking number is reported relative to the identity control, and negation tracking is
itself a measured limitation. The store is near single-class on polarity (92 asserts / 3 denies), so
polarity-stability figures reflect the extractor's default rather than demonstrated polarity reading.
Holdout controls the prompt, not the training data: held-out keys never entered extraction or judging
prompts (measured clean) but may sit in a model's pretraining, which the obfuscation control, showing
identity is not blinded, addresses. Several review packets were judged at the
statement level or from truncated excerpts without full fork-side source text, so those arms are reported
as ``no fork flagged by the scan'', not as validation of the forks. Decisions were model-drafted
suggestions (Claude) reviewed and confirmed by the author, and the open-weight panel leg is not run (no
hosted-open-weight key), so the panel is two-vendor.

\section{Conclusion}
The engine turns cross-theory claim comparison into a reproducible, auditable computation: a typed
intermediate representation, a deterministic judge, and three independent re-expressions of it that agree
exactly. Two lessons generalise beyond this corpus. First, a generator and verifier split pays off when the
verifier is provable and reads only the structured fields, so the generating model has no text channel
through which to argue a verdict into being. Second, honest evaluation here rested on fixed reference
points: the perturbation suite was preregistered and scored against a measured noise floor rather than
against zero; chance rates were estimated by random-key permutation, so that even a below-chance recovery
is reported as one; and every document renders from a single locked registry snapshot, so no number drifts
from one place to the next. The discovery claims themselves are modest by construction: most prior
cross-field correspondences do not survive re-typing, one held-out key is recovered only suggestively, and
the other below chance. I report them that way. The verification lane, not the discovery headline, is the
contribution I stand behind: a claim-comparison verdict that is a checkable function of its inputs.
